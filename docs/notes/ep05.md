## Product segment — merchant cash advance / revenue-based finance
**What it is.** Cash advanced against a merchant's future card receivables. Frequently structured as a *purchase of receivables* rather than a loan — that characterisation drives documentation, accounting and Sharia review.

**Problem solved.** SMEs with no audited accounts and no collateral fail conventional credit. The acquirer already holds the underwriting data (settlement history, seasonality, ticket size, refund rate) *and* controls the repayment channel.

**Money flow.** Advance SR100k → merchant trades → each day the acquirer/PSP diverts a **holdback** (typically 10–20% of that day's settlement, "split funding") to the funder → net settles to the merchant. No direct debit, no instalment date, no missed payment.

**Pricing.** Factor rate, not APR — e.g. take 100, repay 112, flat. Effective cost rises when the merchant trades well (same fee, shorter duration) and falls when trading slows. Duration risk sits with the funder.

**Providers.** Global: Square Loans (Block), Stripe Capital, Shopify Capital, PayPal Working Capital; white-label engines behind banks and marketplaces — Liberis, YouLend, Parafin. Saudi/GCC: merchant-software distribution (e.g. Foodics in F&B), SME debt platforms such as Lendo operating under SAMA's debt-crowdfunding rules, plus consumer players with existing merchant estates.

**Edge cases raised on air.**
- Merchant switches acquirer mid-advance → repayment channel disappears. Needs exclusivity, notice periods, acceleration rights.
- Refunds/chargebacks shrink settlement → holdback shrinks → recovery slows with no credit event.
- A2A / Pay by Bank migration thins the card holdback over time → underwrite *total* settlement and split at the ledger, not at the scheme.

**To offer it, a bank or PSP needs:** a SAMA finance-company permission (or a licensed partner); funding — own book or forward-flow into a vehicle; merchant-level daily settlement splitting that survives reversals; IFRS 9 treatment appropriate to purchased receivables rather than loans; credit-bureau reporting and merchant cost disclosure; and a Sharia structure — true sale of receivables at a discount, or commodity murabaha priced as a profit rate.
